About CKD

What chronic kidney disease is, who is at risk and how it is found.

What the kidneys do

Your two kidneys filter waste and extra fluid out of the blood and pass them out as urine. They also help control blood pressure, keep minerals such as sodium and potassium in balance, and support the production of red blood cells.

What goes wrong

In CKD the kidneys are damaged and gradually lose their ability to filter. It is called chronic when the damage or reduced function lasts three months or longer. Roughly one in ten adults worldwide is estimated to have some degree of CKD, and most do not know it.

Who is at higher risk?

Diabetes

High blood sugar damages the kidney's small filters over time. Diabetes is the leading cause of CKD.

High blood pressure

Raised pressure strains the kidney's blood vessels. It is both a cause and a result of kidney damage.

Family history

Having a close relative with kidney disease raises your own risk.

Older age

Kidney function declines naturally with age, so risk rises after about 60.

Smoking and obesity

Both harm blood vessels and make diabetes and high blood pressure more likely.

Frequent painkillers

Long-term, heavy use of NSAID painkillers such as ibuprofen can injure the kidneys.

Symptoms in early stages

Usually there are none. Most people feel well until a large part of kidney function has been lost, which is why testing people at risk matters.

Symptoms in later stages

  • Tiredness and poor concentration
  • Swollen ankles, feet or hands
  • Itchy skin and muscle cramps
  • Nausea and loss of appetite
  • Changes in how often you pass urine, or foamy urine

How CKD is found

Two simple tests

  • Blood test (eGFR). Serum creatinine is measured and used to estimate the glomerular filtration rate: how much blood the kidneys filter each minute.
  • Urine test (ACR). The albumin-to-creatinine ratio shows whether protein is leaking into the urine, an early sign of damage.

Protecting your kidneys

  • Keep blood pressure and blood sugar within the targets your doctor sets.
  • Eat less salt, stay active and keep a healthy weight.
  • Do not smoke.
  • Use painkillers such as ibuprofen only as directed.
  • If you have diabetes, high blood pressure or a family history of kidney disease, ask for a kidney check (blood and urine test) once a year.

The model

An engineered CatBoost pipeline, tuned to keep sensitivity high on a heavily imbalanced dataset and compared with Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost and SVM.

Research objective

Class imbalance

About 92% of the records are CKD cases. A model can score high accuracy by labelling everyone as CKD, so accuracy alone says little.

Sensitivity first

In screening, a missed case costs more than a false alarm. The study requires at least 92% CKD sensitivity, then looks for the best performance on the minority non-CKD class.

Method

  1. Preprocessing. Identifier and provider columns are removed, values converted to numeric and missing values filled with the median.
  2. Feature selection. Fifteen weak features, identified by agreement between Random Forest, XGBoost and Logistic Regression importances and checked against KDIGO guidance, are dropped.
  3. Stratified split. 70% training, 20% validation and 10% testing, preserving the class ratio in each part.
  4. Imbalance handling. Balanced class weights make errors on the rare non-CKD class count more during training.
  5. Model training. CatBoost (depth 6, learning rate 0.03, L2 regularisation 6, early stopping on validation AUC) and four baselines are trained on the same data.
  6. Threshold optimisation. On the validation set, the decision threshold with the highest balanced accuracy is chosen among those keeping CKD sensitivity at 0.92 or above.
  7. Evaluation. Every model is scored once on the held-out test set with screening metrics, and the CatBoost model is interpreted with feature importance and SHAP values.

      Limitations

      • The analysis uses a single dataset and has not been validated externally or prospectively.
      • The optimised threshold needs to be evaluated on an independent dataset.
      • Feature importance does not establish causal relationships.
      • The model is a research prototype and is not intended for clinical diagnosis or deployment.

      Team and code
